## Executive summary

Ten people were invited, ten interviews were completed and all ten were analyzed, so the campaign met its completion threshold. The picture that emerges is a logistics operation where orders, delivery confirmations and invoices move between systems largely by hand: staff read customer emails and attachments, retype order details into the transport management system, re-key delivery data into billing files, and type supplier invoice data into the finance system. A second recurring theme is approval and status work that lives in email and on paper, with no visible state, so people wait, chase and phone each other. The highest-ranked opportunity is manual keying of PDF supplier invoices into the finance system, raised in 1 of 10 interviews, where document extraction could replace most of the typing. Next is automated extraction of order details from customer emails into the transport management system, raised in 2 of 10 interviews, which touches daily, repetitive work across a customer service team. Third is email-based invoice approval routing without status tracking, raised in 1 of 10 interviews, where a tracked workflow with reminders would replace informal follow-up and reduce missed payment deadlines. Evidence for the first and third of these rests on a single interview each, so breadth across the organization is thin and should be confirmed before any build. Proof-of-delivery capture via the driver app, with signature and photo reaching the transport system within minutes, was described as working well in 3 of 10 interviews and no change is recommended there; several other items, including shift handover and status lookups, are matters of records, rules and integration rather than AI.

**Manual keying of PDF supplier invoices into the finance system.** Incoming supplier and subcontractor invoices arrive as PDF attachments or electronic invoices in a shared accounts payable mailbox, and each PDF is opened and its header and line data typed field by field into the finance system (supplier, invoice number, due date, net amount, VAT, cost centre). Because nothing extracts data from the PDFs, this keying fills a large block of the morning every working day and is rated as substantial pain by the person doing it. Invoices above a value threshold then go out by email for approval and must be waited on before release for payment. A document-extraction capability (OCR plus field recognition) feeding draft invoice records into the finance system, with the handler only reviewing and confirming, would remove most of the typing; routing the approval step through a workflow rather than email would cut the waiting. The flow is described in only a small number of interviews, so the organisational breadth is unclear.

**Automated extraction of order details from customer emails into the transport management system.** Customer transport orders arrive as unstructured emails or spreadsheet attachments, and agents read them, verify addresses and quantity details, and retype the key fields into the transport management system by hand. Participants describe this as a daily, repetitive activity that consumes a notable share of team time, with messy orders taking longer than simple ones and no measured timing available. Mismatches between the attachment and the email text trigger a clarification loop with the customer and a second round of entry. A solution would use document and text extraction to pull pickup and delivery addresses, dates and quantity fields from incoming order emails and attachments, pre-fill a draft order in the system, and leave the agent to confirm or correct it. Validation rules against master data could flag mismatches before the order is created rather than after.

**Consistency check of order details between email text and attached spreadsheet.** Transport orders reach the customer service team by email with a spreadsheet attachment, and the order details such as pickup and delivery addresses and pallet counts must be transferred into the order system. Entering the addresses takes the longest because each value has to be matched carefully against the email text and the attached file. A few times a week the two sources disagree, for example an address component that does not fit together or a differing pallet count, which forces the agent to interrupt entry and go back to the customer for confirmation. A solution would extract the fields from both the email body and the attachment, compare them automatically and flag only the conflicting fields for human confirmation, so the agent no longer has to do the comparison manually. This was described in one interview, so the picture rests on a single perspective.

**Email-based invoice approval routing without status tracking.** Higher-value invoices are sent to managers for approval as email attachments, and the reply email is the only signal that approval has happened. Because there is no approval step or status visible in the finance system, the person handling invoices has to wait several working days, keep track of outstanding approvals informally, and phone managers when nothing has come back by late in the week. Slow turnaround means payment deadlines can be missed, with early-payment discounts lost or late fees incurred. A workflow solution would register each approval request in the finance or workflow system, show who owes a decision, send automatic reminders ahead of the payment deadline, and update the invoice status on approval without manual filing. This is reported in a single interview, so the scope is one role's daily work rather than a broad organizational problem.

**Reactive detection of outgoing invoice errors.** Outgoing customer invoices are not systematically reconciled against the underlying transport and order records before they go out, unlike the checks applied to incoming subcontractor invoices. As a result, wrong or late invoices are usually noticed only when revenue figures look off or a customer or the sales team raises a complaint. The billing team then has to go back manually to the original delivery and order data, identify the discrepancy and issue a corrected invoice or credit note. A proactive reconciliation that compares each outgoing invoice with the recorded delivery and order data, plus an alert for deliveries that have not been invoiced within an expected period, would surface most of these cases before they reach the customer. The fix is largely a matter of deterministic matching rules on data that already exists in the operational system.

**Inbound shipment status enquiries handled by phone.** Customers call in during the day to ask where their shipment is, and staff look the status up in the transport management system while on the phone. The calls cluster in the morning, which creates a queue at the start of the day, and some of the calls are complaints about pickups that ran late rather than simple status questions. A self-service status view or proactive status notifications, fed from the existing a tool, would let customers see progress without calling and would also flag late pickups before the customer has to chase. The remaining calls would then be the genuine exceptions that need a person. The facts here are thin: the work is described as a daily task with no pain reported, so the case rests mainly on the morning queue.

**Manual transfer of completed shipment data into the finance billing file.** At the end of each day, completed shipments are opened one by one in the a tool and their key billing details (shipment identifier, weight and extra charges such as waiting time) are retyped or copied into a separate billing file for finance. Because there is no link between the two systems, every row then has to be checked back against the source before the handoff, which makes the task tedious and error-prone and delays billing until the end of the day. A direct, scheduled transfer of the completed-shipment fields from the a tool into the billing file would remove both the copying and most of the row-by-row verification. The structure is fixed and the fields are few, so rules and an interface are sufficient; no interpretation or prediction is required. The reported effort is about 450 minutes per week in total for this work.

**Repeated manual re-keying of delivery data between operational and billing systems.** Delivery and invoice details are typed again into several systems that are not connected to one another, so the same information is entered two or three times along the chain from dispatch to billing. **Validation of manually keyed amounts and reference numbers.** Amounts, reference numbers and shipment data are entered by hand into the finance system, and a single swapped or wrong digit produces payment corrections and invoices that have to be chased afterwards.
